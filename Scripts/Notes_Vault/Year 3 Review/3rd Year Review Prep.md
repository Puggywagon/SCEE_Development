## Big Picture and Background

Molecular simulation rests on approximating a responsive quantum many-body system with something tractable. Classical force fields make the largest leap: they replace electron distributions that shift with their environment with fixed, pre-assigned atomic charges and simple interaction terms. This is what makes large-scale liquid simulation possible at all — but it has consequences, and knowing when and why these models fail is as important as running them. That matters directly to anyone relying on simulation to predict real, measurable properties.

My main focus, SCEE, addresses the electrostatic consequence of that approximation: molecules cannot polarise in response to their neighbours. Models compensate by folding an average polarisation into inflated charges, which works on average but is not physically faithful. SCEE confronts this directly — by embedding a quantum molecule in a classical bath, it measures the true dipole enhancement a fixed-charge model has to assume rather than derive. At present this work is mainly diagnostic: it maps where that assumption holds or fails across different force fields and molecules. The direction it may take next is to become generative — using what SCEE reveals to inform a new round of PolCA-style improvements, models built to capture the response rather than approximate it away.

Running in parallel, the Zeno line investigation addresses the thermodynamic consequence of the same approximation: because force fields are parameterised against a narrow set of conditions, there is no guarantee they hold across the phase diagram. The Zeno line — the locus where the compressibility factor equals one — is a strikingly regular, experimentally well-characterised feature that emerges from the balance of attraction and repulsion, making it a stringent, near-parameter-free test of a model far from where it was fit.

Both strands probe the same root limitation from different directions — one electrostatic, one thermodynamic — and both tie back to quantities that can be measured: where does the model stop matching reality, and why.

## SCEE Background and Work

SCEE was developed by my group as a QM/MM method for computing the dipole moment of a molecule in a condensed phase accurately but cheaply, by driving the central molecule and its surroundings to a self-consistent _mean_ dipole moment.

The problem it solves — why none of the obvious alternatives suffice:

- Fixed-charge classical force fields carry a fixed dipole, so they cannot respond to a polarising environment and miss the gas→liquid enhancement entirely.
- Full AIMD treats everything quantum-mechanically but is expensive and size/time-limited, and worse, the dipole of a single molecule is ill-defined — you need an arbitrary partitioning of the electron density (Wannier, Bader) that different schemes disagree on.
- Standard electrostatic embedding polarises the central molecule but freezes the surroundings, truncating mutual polarisation at the first step and underestimating the induced dipole by ~0.2 D.
- Full polarised embedding fixes that but is costly and leans heavily on how well the polarisable force field is parameterised.

The key insight that makes SCEE work — and your single best talking point: the central molecule's average dipole depends only on the _mean_ dipole of its surroundings, not on how that polarisation is distributed across individual neighbours. So instead of the full machinery of polarised embedding, you just iterate one number — the mean surrounding dipole — alongside the central molecule's until μ_mm = μ_qm. You verified this isn't a fudge by comparing configurations from a polarisable model (SWM4-DP, every molecule a unique dipole) against fixed-charge configurations (all identical) and getting statistically indistinguishable results. That's the move that buys AIMD-quality polarisation physics at electrostatic-embedding cost.

The second defensible advantage: because the central molecule keeps its own wavefunction — polarised by the surrounding point charges but not electronically entangled with them — its dipole comes straight out of the QM density with no partitioning ambiguity. That's the clean answer to AIMD's biggest weakness.

How it's actually run: classical MD (e.g. TIP4P/2005) generates and samples configurations cheaply across phase space; clusters are extracted and the central molecule treated at QM (B3LYP/aug-cc-pVTZ) via the ONIOM formalism in Gaussian. The LJ part of the ONIOM coupling acts as a repulsive shield stopping the QM atoms collapsing onto bare MM point charges — practically essential (the alcohols wouldn't converge without it) — followed by a bare-point-charge single point to recover the clean electrostatic polarisation.

What it delivers: accuracy comparable to or beyond what AIMD can afford, at far lower cost and over far more state points; dipole distributions largely independent of which MM model generated the configurations (TIP3P the lone outlier), which is your defence against any "isn't this circular" charge; and validated numbers — liquid water ~2.8 D, induced ~0.9 D over the 1.856 D gas value, consistent with both AIMD and the experimental estimates.

Ultimately my goal was to automate SCEE. This has been a big undertaking where the scripts have been through multiple phases of development. Supercritical Water was selected as my starting point for two reasons, beyond what the introduction states. It acted as a good starting point to build up my skills in python and performing simulations, where I have developed code that are the initial building blocks to Automated SCEE. Also there are so many papers on simulating water at ambient conditions (not as much across the phase diagram but still a lot), making it an easy thing to confirm to be working correctly.

The method has been applied across chemically distinct families: liquid water, aliphatic alcohols, and ketones, with aromatic systems now being explored (the V1/V2 scripts). The scientifically interesting result that comes out of spanning those families is that the polarisation splits into two contributions — a _mean-field_ part (the molecule responding to the averaged field of its environment) and a _local_ part (specific near-neighbour interactions, above all hydrogen bonds) — and the balance between them is chemistry-dependent. In hydrogen-bonding liquids like water and alcohols the two contribute roughly equally, so you need both. In ketones, where hydrogen bonds don't form, the mean-field term alone essentially predicts the polarisation. That's a genuine physical insight, not just a benchmark: SCEE is sensitive enough to expose _how_ a given chemistry gets polarised, which is why pushing it onto new functional groups and aromatics is worth doing rather than repetitive.

What automating SCEE brings to the picture:

- Scale. Each new molecule and state point currently means bespoke manual setup. Scripting is what let V0 sweep water across the full phase diagram — dozens of state points, hundreds of configurations — and the same leverage applied generally means far better statistics and far broader coverage than hand-built calculations could ever reach.
- Generality. The V0→V1→V2→V3 trajectory is really a generality ladder: V0 was effectively water-specific, V1/V2 extend to aromatics, and the goal of V3 and beyond is a workflow that handles an arbitrary molecule and its functional groups without rebuilding the pipeline each time.
- Reproducibility and error reduction. The manual route is fiddly — constructing clusters, assigning per-atom charges and LJ parameters, running the self-consistency loop, applying the two-step ONIOM-then-bare-point-charge protocol. Automating it makes the setup consistent and removes a large class of human error.
- Robustness on the awkward steps. A good script has to handle the things that bite manually: the hydroxyl/amino-hydrogen LJ fix that stops convergence failures, detecting gas-phase configurations with no neighbours in the cutoff (and assigning the isolated-molecule dipole), driving the self-consistency to convergence, and the format conversion and output parsing around Gaussian.
- New applications it unlocks. Once it's high-throughput, you can generate dipole-moment distributions across chemical space as _targets_ for parameterising better force fields, and you can feed the broader electrostatics and phase-space studies — including, eventually, the Zeno-line work, which needs many state points to map curvature.

The framing point for the review: automation here isn't "just engineering." It's the step that converts SCEE from a method you apply one careful molecule at a time into a tool that can survey chemistry at scale — which is what makes the force-field-development and thermodynamic-regularity ambitions of the final year actually feasible.
## Zeno line Background

When Z=1 is does not mean the system is behaving like an ideal gas, which is a trap most literature falls into. While the compressibility factor  $Z=\frac{PV}{NK_BT}$ is one for an ideal gas, due to the ideal gas having no interactions. However, in a real fluid two competing effects - attractions between molecules pull them together, pushing Z below 1, while repulsions push Z above 1. The Zeno line represents the set of states where these two effects happen to perfectly balance out. The fluid is dense strong interactions arise which is completely different from an ideal gas, in this case it the system looks ideal in the instance where attraction and repulsion interactions momentarily in balance.

There are two things that make this interesting. The first is the balance does not occur at a single point, but traces across the temperature-density plane. The second is the line is astonishingly straight, which is the empirical regularity (Batschinski's law, $\rho/\rho_B+T/T_B=1$) which is where the literature is interested in. At the two ends are the two anchors on the diagram. At zero density, it hits the temperature axis at the Boyle temperature, the temperature where the second virial coefficient $B_2$ vanishes, resulting in the attraction/repulsion cancellation happens even in the dilute gas. At the other end it descends to $rho_B$, a density you obtain from extrapolating the liquid coexistence curve down to low temperature, sitting near the triple-point liquid density. It passes near the critical point, with the near miss being a means to allow for the estimation of the critical parameters. I have a few papers from people doing this numerically for different materials/atoms. One guy was doing this with uranium and I hope he doesn't get lab time.

Why anyone cares, and this is your bridge to your own work: because Z = 1 marks the attraction-repulsion balance, the position and slope of the Zeno line are a clean fingerprint of the intermolecular potential. Where it sits tells you about the balance of forces in the fluid - and its straightness actually breaks down if the potential is too long- or short-ranged, so deviations are informative too. In the case water, the dominant _attractive_ force is electrostatic - dipole-dipole and hydrogen bonding - and the strength of that attraction depends directly on the molecular dipole moment, the very thing your SCEE work computes and the thing that gets _enhanced_ in the condensed phase by polarisation (1.85 D → ~2.8 D). So the Zeno line of water is, underneath, set by polarisation-enhanced electrostatics.

The Zeno line - the locus in the temperature-density plane along which the compressibility factor equals unity - is one of the more curious regularities in fluid thermodynamics: it is remarkably straight for a wide range of real substances, yet why this should be so remains an open question. Because Z = 1 marks the states where the attractive and repulsive contributions to the pressure exactly cancel, the shape and slope of the line act as a sensitive fingerprint of the underlying intermolecular potential. This is precisely what makes its behaviour in simulation worth studying: common pair potentials such as the Lennard-Jones 12-6 model fail to reproduce the observed linearity, and by systematically tuning the repulsive and attractive character of the interaction -varying the exponents of the Mie potential or the width of a square-well - we can map how the shape of the potential governs the curvature of the line. By pinning down the conditions under which the Zeno line bends, we aim to reason backwards towards why it is so often straight, and to use it as a quantitative target for assessing and refining molecular models. Our longer-term interest is to carry this beyond the simple corresponding-states fluids for which it is best understood into polar, associating systems such as water, where polarisation-enhanced electrostatic interactions reshape the attraction-repulsion balance and tie this strand directly to the group's wider work on condensed-phase electrostatics.
